Question 1:
    I believe the current system is inadequate because it places an unreasonable verification burden on third parties 
and the public. While the city requires registration, the fact that investigations are purely complaint-based 
creates a reactive rather than proactive environment. From a UX and systems design perspective, this relies on 
governance by friction. Airbnb technically displays policy numbers, but without a real-time, automated link to the 
city’s planning database, there is no way for a casual user to know if a number is valid or fabricated. Airbnb doesn't
really enforce locations having the proper buisness liscensing, and it is not a requirement to input liscensing 
information when listing your room. This can be obnoxious for neighbors, and sketchy for Airbnb customers.
    To improve accountability, I believe there should be a mandated API integration between the platform and the city’s 
registration office. Instead of relying on manual scraping by third parties to catch violators, the platform should be 
required to verify license numbers against the city's registry before a listing can even go live. This aligns with the 
SWE Code of Ethics regarding Public Interest, as it ensures that the software platform isn't being used as a tool to 
bypass local housing laws, which contribute to the Bay Area's housing shortage. This forced verification would also make 
Airbnb listings more trustworthy and legal.

Question 2:
    One critical factor is the intent and public-facing nature of the data versus the platform’s Terms of Service (ToS). 
As seen in cases involving the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (the CFAA), there is a fine line between accessing public 
information and exceeding authorized access. I believe a key factor to consider is whether the scraping mimics a human 
user's actions or if it places a burden or overloads the server (like the cyber attacks we've talked about in lecture).
    In this project, for instance, we used static HTML files to avoid violating Airbnb's ToS, which highlights that even 
if data is technically public on a browser, the legal right to extract it at scale is still highly contested. Legality 
should consider whether the scraping is being done for good reason (like holding hosts accountable for rental 
laws) or for a competitive commercial advantage, as the former serves a much higher public utility.

Question 3:
    I think two guidelines would help people evaluate the ethical risks of webscraping.
      One would be the principle of individual privacy expectation. Which is that before scraping, one must question if the 
        person who posted this data expects it to be used in this way. Even if data is public (like a social media post or an 
        Airbnb listing), the user may not have consented to having their data aggregated, deanonymized, or used for algorithmic 
        profiling. If the scraping could lead to the identification of private individuals who didn't intend to be public figures, 
        it should be avoided or strictly anonymized.
      The other would be the proportionality of social benefit. Which is that there should be a clear, positive social outcome  
        that outweighs the potential harm to the platform or the users' privacy. In this Airbnb project, the social benefit is  
        protecting the local housing market from illegal rentals. However, if the scraping is merely to harvest emails for marketing  
        or to create a secondary "shadow" database of users without their knowledge, the ethical cost is too high compared to the 
        benefit.
