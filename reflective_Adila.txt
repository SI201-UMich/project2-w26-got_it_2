1) I do not think the current system provides adequate accountability around short-term rentals in 
San Francisco. One of the main reasons is that, in this project, we had to determine whether a listing’s 
policy number was valid by extracting the data ourselves through web scraping. In doing so, we found 
listings where the policy number was not valid as it was neither “Pending,” “Exempt,” nor in a valid 
format yet the listing was still available on the platform. This shows that the system does not effectively 
enforce compliance. Additionally, the policy numbers were not easily visible and had to be extracted from 
individual listing pages which suggests a lack of transparency. If users cannot easily verify whether a 
listing is legally registered, it becomes difficult to hold hosts accountable. According to the SWE Code of 
Ethics, software engineers should ensure that their products meet high professional standards and 
prioritize the public good. Allowing listings with unclear or invalid policy numbers to remain active 
does not meet these standards as it may mislead users and create potential safety or legal concerns. 
To improve accountability, Airbnb should require hosts to submit verified policy numbers before allowing 
listings to be posted. The platform should also clearly display these numbers on the main listing pages so 
users can easily access them. This would increase trust between users and the platform.


2) After reading the articles, one important factor to consider when discussing the legality of web 
scraping is whether websites clearly communicate their rules and restrictions regarding scraping. 
In many cases, legality depends on whether a user is violating a website’s terms of service or accessing 
data in an unauthorized way. However, these terms are often long, unclear, or do not explicitly address 
web scraping. For example, in the case where researchers created fake profiles to test discrimination, 
the judge ruled that their actions did not violate the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) because the 
website did not clearly prohibit the use of fake identities. This shows that when guidelines are vague or 
missing, it becomes difficult to determine what counts as “unauthorized access.” Because of this, having a 
clear and accessible section specifically addressing web scraping on websites would help both users and 
companies to ensure the legality of web scraping being honored. So, it would reduce ambiguity and make it
easier to determine whether scraping is legal or not.

3) Two guidelines that are important to consider when deciding whether to perform web scraping are protecting user 
privacy and minimizing harm to the platform being scraped.
    Web scraping should not expose or misuse sensitive or personal information even if that data is publicly available. Just because
    information is accessible online does not mean it is ethical to collect and analyze it at scale. For example, 
    scandals like Facebook–Cambridge Analytica show how personal data can be used in ways that harm individuals without 
    their consent. Similar to how HIPAA protects sensitive health information, scraping practices should avoid collecting 
    identifiable or sensitive data and should ensure that users are not negatively impacted.
    
    Web scraping should not harm the functionality or performance of the website being scraped. Sending a 
    large number of automated requests can overload servers, slow down services and negatively affect real 
    users trying to access the site. Ethical scraping should avoid excessive traffic, and ensure that the 
    platform and its users are not disrupted.
